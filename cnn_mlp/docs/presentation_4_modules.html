Tokamak Physics 101

Tokamak Confinement in 60 Seconds: Why Centimeters Matter

The physical setup behind MAST and Task 1-3 equilibrium reconstruction

Nuclear fusion powers the Sun. On Earth, we heat hydrogen gas to 100,000,000°C—ten times hotter than the Sun's core. At this extreme temperature, atoms strip into a boiling soup of ions called plasma.

The Magnetic Cage
  • No physical material can touch 100M°C plasma without instantly melting.
  • We trap the plasma inside a donut-shaped vacuum vessel using invisible magnetic fields generated by giant copper coils.
  • The Output: Our AI predicts a 2D cross-section map of magnetic flux $\psi(R, Z)$, which tells operators where the plasma is located every millisecond.
The Golden Rule of Tokamak Safety

The plasma must never touch the steel wall. A drift of just 3 to 5 cm can scrape the beryllium tiles, vaporizing metal, contaminating the plasma, and shutting down the reactor.

Doubt #1 • The NRMSE Paradox

Why NRMSE Lies: The 75% Cold Vacuum Trap

Why traditional computer vision loss is useless for nuclear fusion safety

Your Exact Doubt from the Text File

"The NRMSE compares actual vs predicted pixel values across all 4,225 pixels. Shouldn't it indirectly take the center and boundary into consideration? Why doesn't it see the drift?"

The Face Analogy:
Imagine a passport photo of 4,225 pixels. 3,500 pixels are a plain white background wall. Only 700 pixels are the person's face. If the eyes and nose shift 2 inches to the left, 85% of the pixels still match the white wall! The Mean Squared Error is tiny (0.02). But the person looks deformed!
The Physics Reality
  • In our $65 \times 65$ grid, over 75% of the pixels represent the cold, empty vacuum near the vessel wall where $\psi \approx 0$.
  • If the hot core drifts by 3 cm toward the wall, the empty vacuum pixels still match almost zero. The average NRMSE barely changes from 0.141 to 0.145!
  • A computer vision engineer looks at 0.145 and says: "Great model! Low error!"
  • A nuclear fusion engineer looks at the actual reactor and screams: "The machine just melted!"
The Solution (Module 1)

Discard pixel-averaged loss as the primary safety metric. Calculate the true physical shift in centimeters (cm) at the boundary and the core.

Doubt #2 • Grid Geometry

How Do Pixels Convert to Physical Centimeters?

Uncovering the exact coordinate system of the 65x65 TokaMark grid

Your Question

"How can pixel values correspond to cm in some way? An image consists of infinitely small continuous points. Did we sample discrete points, and how do they map to physical distances?"

The Key Revelation: The output grid is not a camera photograph. It is a spatial coordinate map representing a 2-meter physical slice of the MAST tokamak chamber!
The Exact Physical Geometry
  • Horizontal Axis $R$ (Radius from center column):
    Spans from $R = 0.2\text{ m}$ ($20\text{ cm}$) to $R = 2.0\text{ m}$ ($200\text{ cm}$).
    Total physical width = $1.8\text{ m} = 180\text{ cm}$.
  • Vertical Axis $Z$ (Height from machine midplane):
    Spans from $Z = -1.5\text{ m}$ ($-150\text{ cm}$) to $Z = +1.5\text{ m}$ ($+150\text{ cm}$).
    Total physical height = $3.0\text{ m} = 300\text{ cm}$.
The Simple Conversion Formula

Across $65$ grid points, there are exactly $64$ intervals:

Radial Step $\Delta R$
$180\text{ cm} / 64 = \mathbf{2.8125\text{ cm / pixel}}$
Vertical Step $\Delta Z$
$300\text{ cm} / 64 = \mathbf{4.6875\text{ cm / pixel}}$

If the AI predicts the center is 1 pixel off vertically, that is an error of 4.69 cm in real life!

Module 1 Deep Dive

Module 1: The Physics-Aware Evaluation Layer

Four physical safety metrics that replace naive pixel NRMSE

1. Plasma Boundary Error (LCFS)

What is it: The distance between the predicted and true outermost edge of the plasma (Last Closed Flux Surface).

How we extract it: Run standard Marching Squares contour extraction at $\psi = \psi_{\text{LCFS}}$ on both grids, then compute the average distance between the contours in centimeters.

2. Magnetic Axis Shift

What is it: The shift in the hottest, densest center of the plasma core.

How we extract it: Find the absolute peak $(r, z) = \arg\max \psi$ in the 2D grid, refine it with sub-pixel 2D quadratic interpolation, and calculate Euclidean distance in centimeters.

3. X-Point Location Error

What is it: The exhaust funnel position at the bottom of diverted shots.

How we extract it: Find the saddle point where the magnetic gradient vanishes ($\nabla \psi = 0$) in the lower divertor region, and measure distance in centimeters.

4. False Wall-Proximity Alarm Rate

What is it: How often the AI mistakenly sounds an emergency abort because it thought the plasma was touching the wall.

How we calculate it: Measure minimum wall distance. If $d_{\text{pred}} < 5\text{ cm}$ while $d_{\text{true}} \ge 5\text{ cm}$, record a False Alarm.

Doubt #3 • Physical Locations

Does the Center Move? Does an X-Point Actually Exist?

Resolving whether these points exist in the TokaMark 65x65 data

Question 1: Is the center fixed?

"Is the center fixed or what? Like how do we get the center in this exactly?"

Answer: The center is never fixed! The plasma is a fluid that shifts, breathes, and wobbles every millisecond due to changing coil currents. The magnetic axis is the dynamic peak of magnetic flux $\psi$ at each time step.
Question 2: Does an X-point exist in the 65x65 grid?

"Is an X-point even there in the 65x65 true grid? I didn't see it anywhere on the heatmap, does it even exist?"

Answer: YES! In diverted shots, the bottom of the plasma forms a sharp "X" shape. At that exact point, poloidal magnetic field $B_p = 0$, meaning the gradient $\nabla \psi = 0$ is a mathematical saddle point!
The Calculus Behind Saddle Point Detection

In multivariable calculus, a saddle point is where slopes in both directions are flat ($\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial R} = 0$ and $\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial Z} = 0$), but the surface curves up in one direction and down in the other ($\det(\text{Hessian}) < 0$).

We can compute this in pure NumPy using np.gradient() in 5 lines of code. It is 100% extractable from the 65x65 grid!

Doubt #4 • Emergency Aborts

How Do Real Tokamaks Abort, and How Do We Measure Alarms?

Connecting neural network output to reactor emergency systems

Your Question on Aborts

"How does a reactor suddenly abort when plasma is already formed and floating in the air? And are we testing an alarm here, or is it part of reconstruction?"

How Real Aborts Work:
If an emergency abort is triggered, the tokamak fires Shattered Pellet Injection (SPI): frozen pellets of cryogenic neon gas are shot by pneumatic guns into the plasma in < 10 ms. The neon freezes the plasma and safely radiates the energy away before it strikes the wall!
Why False vs Missed Alarms Matter
  • False Alarm (The Boy Who Cried Wolf): The AI panics and thinks plasma is at 3 cm (touching wall), but it was actually at 12 cm (safe). The emergency system injects neon, killing a $50,000 experimental shot for nothing.
  • Missed Alarm (Lethal Silence): The AI predicts plasma is at 8 cm (safe), but in reality it drifted to 2 cm. No abort is triggered, plasma slams into the wall, vaporizing tungsten tiles and costing millions in repairs.
How We Compute It in Our Project

We are not building an alarm bell hardware device! We evaluate our 2D predictions: for every test window, we measure the minimum distance from predicted boundary to the wall. If $d_{\text{pred}} < 5\text{ cm}$ while $d_{\text{true}} \ge 5\text{ cm}$, we increment our False Alarm Counter.

Module 2: Failure-Aware & Physics-Constrained AI

Training the neural network so it expects broken sensors and obeys physics

The Problem with the Baseline

The standard TokaMark CNN was trained on 100% pristine, perfect data.

When Probe #7 dies during inference and sends $0.0$, the baseline assumes the magnetic field at that location is literally zero! It warps the entire 2D donut.

The Two Innovations in Module 2
  • 1. Missingness Mask Concatenation: Feed a companion binary mask vector $M \in \{0, 1\}^{40}$ alongside the sensor data.
  • 2. Structured Failure Training: During training, deliberately corrupt random sensors and flip their mask bit to $0$. The model learns: "Ignore dead channel 7; infer flux from the surrounding active coils!"
  • 3. Flux-Loop Consistency PINN Loss: Force the predicted 2D grid values at the wall to match the real flux loop measurements!
The Driving Analogy: If your car speedometer breaks while driving on the highway, you don't panic and crash. You look at the tachometer (engine RPM) and the flow of traffic to infer your speed. Masked training teaches the AI to do the exact same thing!
Doubt #5 • Masked Training

How Does Masked Training Work? Is It Heavy?

Dispelling confusion about Masked Autoencoders (MAE)

Your Question on Masking

"We used a masked autoencoder to train it by corrupting random sensors... basically denoising autoencoder? How does it handle loss of sensors data, and is a masked encoder needed at inference?"

Clarification: We do NOT need a heavy, slow Vision Masked Autoencoder (MAE)! That would require massive memory and compute that our setup cannot afford.
The Lightweight, Elegant Solution
  • We use Input Mask Concatenation & Structured Dropout:
  • Input tensor shape becomes $(B, 40 \times 2, 5)$ instead of $(B, 40, 5)$:
    • Top 40 channels: Raw sensor signals.
    • Bottom 40 channels: Binary mask (1 = Healthy, 0 = Dead/Corrupt).
  • During training, randomly zero out 1 to 6 sensor channels and set their mask bits to 0.
  • At inference time: When our fault detector notices Sensor 4 has flatlined, it sets $\text{Mask}[4] = 0$. The model immediately knows: "Sensor 4 is dead, don't trust it!"
Why This Is Pure Deep Learning Novelty

Standard TokaMark blindly feeds numbers into a CNN. Our failure-aware architecture explicitly conditions the network on diagnostic health state, making it inherently resilient to hardware degradation.

Doubt #6 • The Big Unit Mystery

Resolving the Unit Mystery: Voltages vs Webers

How can we compare flux loop measurements directly to the 2D output grid?

Your Exact Question (The Biggest Mystery)

"How will comparing predicted field to physical voltage work? You can't directly compare grid values to voltage because they are not of the same unit! Or do you mean we ensure our predicted value matches the actual image?"

The Incredible Revelation: The Units Match!
  • In physical hardware, wire loops pick up induced voltage ($V = -d\Phi/dt$).
  • However, in the MAST data acquisition pipeline, these raw voltages pass through electronic analog/digital integrators ($\psi = \int V dt$).
  • In the official TokaMark dataset, the channel magnetics/flux_loop_flux is ALREADY integrated and stored in Webers (Wb)!
  • And what is our 2D output grid $\psi(R, Z)$? It is poloidal magnetic flux in Webers (Wb)!
  • They are the EXACT same physical quantity in the EXACT same physical units (Webers)!
Do We Have the Coordinates of the 15 Loops?

YES! The 15 flux loops are permanently welded to the inner vacuum wall at fixed physical coordinates $(R_k, Z_k)$. We know the exact position of every single loop around the perimeter.

Module 2 • PINN Constraint

The Flux-Loop Consistency PINN Loss

Anchoring internal AI predictions to real-world boundary measurements

A standard neural network is an unconstrained function approximator. It can hallucinate smooth 2D heatmaps that look pretty on paper but completely contradict physical reality at the boundary.

The PINN Boundary Formula

For each of the 15 flux loops at known wall coordinates $(R_k, Z_k)$:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{FL}} = \frac{1}{15} \sum_{k=1}^{15} \Big( \hat{\psi}(R_k, Z_k) - y_{\text{measured}, k} \Big)^2$$

$\hat{\psi}(R_k, Z_k)$ is sampled from the AI's predicted 2D grid using bilinear interpolation.

Total Loss Function
$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}} + \lambda_{\text{FL}} \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{FL}}$$

Where $\lambda_{\text{FL}} \approx 0.1$ balances global reconstruction with strict boundary anchoring.

PHYSICAL INTUITION
The 15 Thermometers Analogy:
Imagine trying to predict the 3D temperature map of a giant furnace. You have 15 thermometers bolted directly to the outside casing.

If your AI predicts that the inside is 500°C, but right where Thermometer #3 is bolted the AI predicts 100°C while the thermometer reads 350°C—your AI is violating physics!

The PINN loss penalizes the AI until its boundary predictions match the real thermometers.

Answering the multi-million dollar fusion reactor design question

The Fusion Cost Reality: Drilling holes into a vacuum vessel to mount radiation-hardened sensors costs hundreds of thousands of dollars per sensor. Finding the minimal set directly reduces reactor build costs!
The Greedy Backward Pruning Algorithm
  • Step 1: Start with all 40 sensors active ($\text{Boundary Error} \approx 0.9\text{ cm}$).
  • Step 2: Set the maximum safe tolerance: $\text{Error} \le \mathbf{2.0\text{ cm}}$.
  • Step 3: In each round, test turning off each remaining sensor and pick the one that causes the smallest error increase. Remove it!
  • Step 4: Repeat until turning off any more sensors would push the error above $2.0\text{ cm}$.

Doubt #8 • Routing Logic

Clearing Doubts on Time Windows & Virtual Sensors

Resolving the 5 ms window question and spatial neighbor interpolation

Question 1: Isn't our window 5 ms?

"If it's momentary change < 10 ms, isn't our window frame 5 ms? For each 5 ms window we are outputting right?"

Answer: Each window contains 5 discrete time steps sampled at 1 kHz ($\Delta t = 1\text{ ms}$, total window = 5 ms). If a spike or drop lasts for only 1 or 2 steps (< 2 ms), that is a transient noise glitch! A simple temporal forward-fill ($s(t) = s(t-1)$) fixes it instantly with zero latency.
Question 2: Are any sensors actually redundant?

"We estimate values from neighbors... but nothing would be useless to begin with right?"

Answer: No sensor is "useless", but many are physically redundant! Magnetic probes are bolted right next to each other around the wall. If Probe #4 dies, Probe #3 and Probe #5 are only 15 cm away. Because magnetic fields vary smoothly in space, $(s_3 + s_5)/2$ provides an excellent approximation!
When Do We Route to Module 2?

If a unique, isolated, highly critical sensor dies (like a primary flux loop with no close neighbor), neighbor interpolation is too inaccurate. That is when the router sends the masked input directly to our Module 2 Failure-Aware AI!

Calibrated Confidence: The AI Safety Gauge

How conformal prediction tells controllers when not to trust the model

Your Question on Confidence

"Along with the 2D flux map, the AI outputs an uncertainty bound too. If it goes below a threshold we say to not trust it? How does this work?"

The Pilot Analogy:
An automated flight system doesn't just say "altimeter reads 200 feet." In heavy fog with one sensor glitching, it says "altimeter reads 200 feet ± 80 feet." That uncertainty bound tells the pilot: "Pull up immediately!"
How Conformal Prediction Works in Practice
  • We calculate the prediction residual error distribution on a held-out calibration set.
  • At inference time, the model outputs: $$\text{Plasma Boundary Position} \pm \delta\text{ cm}$$
  • Clean Sensors: Uncertainty is tight ($\delta \approx \mathbf{0.5\text{ cm}}$). Controller confidence: HIGH.
  • 3 Sensors Dead: Uncertainty expands ($\delta \approx \mathbf{3.8\text{ cm}}$).
  • If $[\text{Boundary} + \delta]$ touches the physical vessel wall, the system outputs:
    STATUS: UNCERTAIN • INITIATE CONSERVATIVE COIL BACKUP
